Question No. 6General Intelligence and Reasoning (Q.6)

A)
4% increase
B)

D)

Correct Option: B) 4% decrease
Explanation:

Given: Let the original number = 100

According to Question: The number is increased by 20% and then decreased by 20%.

Increase = 20% of 100
20% of 100 = (20 / 100) × 100 = 20

So, new number after increase = 100 + 20 = 120

Decrease = 20% of 120
20% of 120 = (20 / 100) × 120 = 24

So, new number after decrease = 120 − 24 = 96

Net change = 100 − 96 = 4 (decrease)

Hence, the correct answer is Option 2.

📌 Key Points

  • Statement 1: The amalaka is a distinctive, circular, ribbed stone disk that sits atop the shikhara (tower) of Nagara-style temples. This is a defining feature of the Nagara temple architecture.
  • The amalaka symbolizes the celestial world and serves as a crowning feature of the shikhara, emphasizing verticality and spiritual connection.
  • Statement 2: In Drāviḍa-style temples, the vimana refers to the tower or superstructure directly above the sanctum (garbhagriha), not the gateway tower (gopuram). The gopuram refers to the ornate gateway towers found predominantly in Drāviḍa temples.
  • The confusion arises because both the vimana and gopuram are tall structures, but their functions and locations within the temple complex differ.
  • Therefore, Statement 1 is correct, while Statement 2 is incorrect.

📌 Additional Information

  • Nagara Style of Temple Architecture:
    • The Nagara style is primarily found in northern India and is characterized by its curvilinear shikhara (tower).
    • The amalaka and kalasha are prominent features atop the shikhara.
    • The temples have a square base and are often built on a raised platform called a jagati.
    • Examples include the Kandariya Mahadev Temple in Khajuraho and the Sun Temple in Konark.
  • Drāviḍa Style of Temple Architecture:
    • The Drāviḍa style is predominant in southern India and is known for its pyramidal vimanas above the sanctum.
    • The gopurams, or gateway towers, are a unique feature of this style and are often more prominent than the vimana itself.
    • The temples are enclosed within walls, with multiple gopurams marking the entrances.
    • Examples include the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur and the Meenakshi Temple in Madurai.
  • Key Differences Between Nagara and Drāviḍa Styles:
    • The Nagara style emphasizes verticality with curvilinear shikharas, while the Drāviḍa style has tiered and pyramidal vimanas.
    • Gopurams are a hallmark of the Drāviḍa style, but they are absent in the Nagara style.
    • The Nagara temples are relatively smaller and lack boundary walls, unlike the enclosed complexes of Drāviḍa temples.
  • Additional Terminologies:
    • Shikhara: The tower or spire above the sanctum in Nagara-style temples.
    • Amalaka: The circular, ribbed stone disk atop the shikhara, symbolizing the divine.
    • Vimana: The pyramidal structure above the sanctum in Drāviḍa-style temples.
    • Gopuram: Ornate gateway towers, typically found in Drāviḍa temple complexes.
    • Garbhagriha: The innermost sanctum of a temple where the deity resides.

  • Each point on the curve indicates that the consumer is indifferent between the combinations of goods, meaning they derive equal utility from them.
  • Indifference curves are typically downward sloping, reflecting the trade-offs between goods, and they do not intersect.
  • The slope of the indifference curve, known as the Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS), measures the rate at which a consumer is willing to give up one good for another while maintaining the same utility level.

  • Budget Constraint:
    • A budget constraint represents the combinations of goods and services a consumer can purchase given their income and the prices of the goods.
    • It is a straight line that shows the trade-off between two goods, limited by the consumer's financial resources.
    • The slope of the budget constraint is determined by the ratio of the prices of the two goods.
    • While the budget constraint determines what is affordable, it does not indicate preferences or satisfaction like an indifference curve does.
  • Isoquant Curve:
    • An isoquant curve represents all combinations of inputs (e.g., labor and capital) that produce the same level of output.
    • It is used in production theory to analyze input combinations and efficiency, not consumer preferences.
    • Like indifference curves, isoquants are downward sloping, but they are specific to production rather than utility.
    • The slope of an isoquant curve reflects the Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution (MRTS), which measures the rate at which one input can be substituted for another while maintaining the same output level.
  • Marginal Cost Curve:
    • The marginal cost curve shows the additional cost incurred for producing one more unit of a good or service.
    • It is a key concept in production and cost analysis, helping firms determine optimal production levels.

    • The marginal cost curve typically intersects with the average cost curve at its minimum point.

    • The indifference curve is a fundamental concept in consumer choice theory, which analyzes how consumers make decisions to maximize their satisfaction given constraints such as income and prices.
