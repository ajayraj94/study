A)
Karna Sringa

Correct Option: D) Bhadra
Explanation:

The correct answer is Bhadra.

📌 Key Points

  • Bhadra is the projecting central offset of the wall face in the Nagara-style temples of Indian architecture.
  • This feature is prominent in Nagara temple architecture, which is prevalent in North India.
  • The term "Bhadra" specifically refers to the central projection of the temple's walls, aligned with the garbhagriha (sanctum sanctorum).
  • The projection often has intricate carvings and is considered a significant part of the temple's exterior design, enhancing its aesthetic appeal and symmetry.
  • Nagara architecture is characterized by its curvilinear towers (shikharas) and distinct, well-defined geometrical patterns, with Bhadra being one of its key elements.

📌 Additional Information

  • Nagara Temple Architecture:
    • The Nagara style is one of the three main types of temple architecture in India, the other two being Dravidian (South India) and Vesara (mixed style).
    • Nagara temples are mostly found in North and Central India, with prominent examples in Odisha, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh.
    • The shikhara (tower) of Nagara temples is usually curvilinear and rises vertically in a pyramidal form.
    • The temples are constructed on a raised platform called a jagati.
    • The garbhagriha (sanctum) is the most sacred part of the temple, housing the deity.
  • Other Elements of Nagara Architecture:
    • Karna Sringa: It refers to the corner projections or miniature spires adorning the shikhara of the temple.
    • Pratiratha: These are the secondary projections or offsets on the temple walls, smaller than the central projection (Bhadra).
    • Anuratha: These are minor offsets or projections, even smaller than the Pratiratha, adding to the temple's decorative detailing.
  • Significance of Bhadra:
    • The Bhadra projection aligns with the entrance to the sanctum and often features elaborate carvings depicting mythological scenes or religious motifs.
    • It serves both an aesthetic and functional purpose, enhancing the overall appearance of the temple and aiding in the structural balance of the design.

📌 Important Points

  • Examples of Nagara Temples:
    • Sun Temple, Konark: Located in Odisha, it is a classic example of Nagara architecture with its chariot-shaped design.
    • Kandariya Mahadev Temple: Situated in Khajuraho, Madhya Pradesh, it showcases the intricate carvings and towering shikhara typical of Nagara temples.
    • Jagdish Temple: Located in Udaipur, Rajasthan, this temple exhibits the symmetrical and geometrical design features of the Nagara style.
  • Role of Projections in Temple Design:
    • Projections like Bhadra, Pratiratha, and Anuratha contribute to the three-dimensional appearance of the temple.
    • They enhance the play of light and shadow, creating a dynamic visual effect on the temple's surface.

📌 Key Points

  • Assertion (A): Gothic Revival buildings in colonial India often employed pointed arches and ribbed vaults.
    • The statement is true. Gothic Revival architecture in colonial India drew heavily from European Gothic styles, incorporating features such as pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses, and stained glass windows.
    • Examples of Gothic Revival structures in India include the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus (formerly Victoria Terminus) in Mumbai and the St. Paul’s Cathedral in Kolkata.
    • This architectural style was particularly popular during the 19th century, reflecting the British colonial influence and their admiration for medieval European architecture.
  • Reason (R): These elements were unknown in traditional European cathedrals.
    • The statement is false. Pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and other Gothic elements originated in medieval European architecture and were hallmarks of the Gothic style, which flourished in Europe from the 12th to the 16th centuries.
    • Major examples of Gothic architecture in Europe include the Notre-Dame Cathedral in Paris and the Westminster Abbey in London.
    • Thus, these elements were well-known in European cathedrals and were not unique to colonial India.
  • The correct explanation for the use of Gothic Revival architecture in colonial India lies in the British desire to symbolize power and cultural superiority by blending traditional European styles with local Indian aesthetics.
  • Conclusion: Since Assertion (A) is true but Reason (R) is false, the correct answer is 3) A is true, but R is false.

📌 Additional Information

  • Gothic Revival Architecture:
    • Gothic Revival was an architectural movement that began in the late 18th century in England and spread to other parts of the world, including colonial India.
    • The style is characterized by pointed arches, ribbed vaults, intricate tracery, and verticality in design.
    • It was often used for religious, educational, and administrative buildings.
  • Key Features of Gothic Architecture:
    • Pointed Arches: These allowed for taller and more stable structures.
    • Ribbed Vaults: Provided structural support and allowed for elaborate ceiling designs.
    • Flying Buttresses: Supported walls and enabled the construction of large stained glass windows.
    • Ornamentation: Included gargoyles, pinnacles, and tracery.
  • Examples of Gothic Revival in India:
    • Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus: A UNESCO World Heritage Site in Mumbai, designed by Frederick William Stevens.
    • St. Paul’s Cathedral: Located in Kolkata, this is one of the finest examples of Indo-Gothic architecture.
    • University of Bombay: Features the Rajabai Clock Tower, inspired by the Big Ben in London.
  • Historical Context:
    • The British introduced Gothic Revival in India during the 19th century to showcase their cultural heritage while adapting to the Indian climate and materials.
    • This style was often used for government buildings, churches, and educational institutions.

📌 Important Points

  • Medieval Gothic Architecture:
    • Originated in the 12th century in France and spread across Europe.
    • Key examples include the Chartres Cathedral and the Amiens Cathedral.
    • The style is associated with the Age of Cathedrals and the rise of cities.
  • Indo-Gothic Style:
    • A blend of European Gothic elements with Indian architectural features.
    • It often included local materials and adapted to the Indian climate.
    • Examples include the Madras High Court and the Government Museum in Chennai.

  • If the source and the listener move with the same speed in the same direction, the relative velocity between them remains constant. This means there is no relative motion between the source and the listener in the direction of the wave.
  • Since the relative velocity is zero, the wavelength and frequency of the sound wave as perceived by the listener do not change. The frequency remains the same as that emitted by the source.
  • The Doppler Shift formula for perceived frequency is given by:
    f' = f * (v + vₒ) / (v + vₛ)
    Where:
    - f' is the perceived frequency.
    - f is the actual frequency of the source.
    - v is the speed of sound in the medium.
    - vₒ is the velocity of the observer.
    - vₛ is the velocity of the source.
    If vₒ = vₛ, then the numerator and denominator are equal, resulting in f' = f.
  • Hence, the perceived frequency remains unchanged when both the source and the listener move at the same speed in the same direction.

📌 Additional Information

    • The Doppler Effect is the change in the frequency or wavelength of a wave in relation to an observer moving relative to the source of the wave.
    • It applies to all types of waves, including sound waves, light waves, and electromagnetic waves.
    • The effect is commonly observed in sound waves, such as the pitch of a siren changing as it moves towards or away from an observer.
  • Stationary Source and Observer:

  • Applications of Doppler Effect:
