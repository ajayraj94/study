Which dynasty ruled over Kannauj during the Tripartite Struggle?
A)
Rashtrakutas
B)
Chalukyas
C)
Gurjara-Pratiharas  ✓ (Correct Answer)
D)
Palas - Cholas
Correct Option: C) Gurjara-Pratiharas
Explanation:
The correct answer is Gurjara-Pratiharas.

📌 Key Points

  • The Tripartite Struggle was a significant historical conflict that took place in North India between the 8th century and the 10th century.
  • The struggle was centered around the control of the city of Kannauj, which was considered a symbol of prestige and sovereign power after the death of Harshavardhana.
  • Three major dynasties were involved in this long-standing conflict: the Gurjara-Pratiharas of Western India, the Palas of Bengal, and the Rashtrakutas of the Deccan.
  • The Gurjara-Pratiharas ultimately managed to establish long-term control over Kannauj. During the reign of Nagabhata II (around 816 AD), the dynasty successfully conquered Kannauj and made it their capital city. Hence, the Gurjara-Pratiharas are the correct answer as they ruled over Kannauj as their primary seat of power during this era.
  • The conflict initially began when the Pratihara king Vatsaraja defeated the Pala ruler Dharmapala and the Rashtrakuta king Dhruva Dharavarsha intervened, defeating both.

📌 Additional Information

  • Significance of Kannauj:
    • Located in the Ganga valley in present-day Uttar Pradesh, Kannauj was strategically important for trade and agriculture.
    • Possession of Kannauj signified lordship over the Indo-Gangetic plains, leading to its nickname as the "Mahodaya Nagar".
  • The Palas:
    • Founded by Gopala in 750 AD, this dynasty ruled over Bengal and Bihar.
    • Dharmapala and Devapala were the most powerful rulers who briefly installed their nominees on the throne of Kannauj during the struggle.
  • The Rashtrakutas:
    • Founded by Dantidurga in 753 AD with their capital at Manyakheta in the Deccan region (modern-day Karnataka).
    • Rulers like Dhruva, Govinda III, and Indra III frequently invaded North India and defeated the Pratiharas and Palas, but they often returned south, allowing the Pratiharas to regain control.
  • The Chalukyas and Cholas:
    • The Chalukyas of Badami had declined by the mid-8th century, and the Chalukyas of Kalyani rose later; they were not primary participants in the Tripartite Struggle.
    • The Cholas were a powerful maritime empire in South India that reached its peak under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I in the 11th century, well after the core period of the Tripartite Struggle.

📌 Important Points

  • Consequences of the Struggle:
    • The constant warfare for over two centuries exhausted the resources of all three dynasties, leading to their eventual fragmentation and decline.
    • The weakness of these empires paved the way for the rise of small Rajput principalities and later facilitated the Ghaznavid and Ghurid invasions of India.
  • Mihira Bhoja:
    • The Gurjara-Pratihara ruler Mihira Bhoja (836–885 AD) was one of the most formidable monarchs of this period. He consolidated Kannauj and successfully defended his territory against both the Palas and the Rashtrakutas.
    • The Arab traveler Al-Masudi referred to the Gurjara-Pratihara kingdom as Al-Juzr and praised their powerful cavalry.

As per Article ___________ of the Indian Constitution, from the date of commencement of the Constitution (Sixty-ninth Amendment) Act, 1991, the Union territory of Delhi shall be called the National Capital Territory of Delhi.

Explanation:

The correct answer is 239AA.

📌 Key Points

  • Article 239AA of the Indian Constitution was inserted by the Constitution (Sixty-ninth Amendment) Act, 1991. It conferred a special status on the Union Territory of Delhi, designating it as the National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi.
  • As per this article, the Administrator of the National Capital Territory of Delhi, appointed under Article 239, shall be designated as the Lieutenant Governor.
  • The article provided for the creation of a Legislative Assembly for the NCT of Delhi, the members of which are chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies in the Union Territory.
  • The Legislative Assembly has the power to make laws for the whole or any part of the NCT of Delhi with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the State List or Concurrent List, except for three specific subjects: Public Order, Police, and Land.
  • These provisions officially came into force on February 1, 1992, marking a significant shift in the administrative and political structure of the capital city of India.

📌 Additional Information

  • Article 239AB: This article deals with the failure of constitutional machinery in the NCT of Delhi. It allows the President, on receiving a report from the Lieutenant Governor, to suspend the operation of any provision of Article 239AA if the administration of the territory cannot be carried on in accordance with the law.
  • Article 239: This is the general provision for the administration of Union Territories. It states that every Union Territory shall be administered by the President acting, to such extent as he thinks fit, through an administrator appointed by him with such designation as he may specify.
  • Article 239A: This article was inserted by the Constitution (Fourteenth Amendment) Act, 1962, to create local Legislatures or Council of Ministers or both for certain Union Territories like Puducherry.
  • Article 239B: Grants the Administrator of certain Union Territories the power to promulgate ordinances during the recess of the Legislature.
  • The 69th Amendment Act was based on the recommendations of the S. Balakrishnan Committee, which was set up in 1987 to look into the demands for statehood for Delhi and suggested a compromise of a Legislative Assembly with limited powers.

📌 Important Points

  • Composition of the Assembly: The Legislative Assembly of Delhi consists of 70 members. The total number of seats is determined by Parliament by law.
  • Council of Ministers: Article 239AA(4) provides for a Council of Ministers consisting of not more than 10% of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly (which equals 7 ministers, including the Chief Minister), to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor in the exercise of his functions.
  • Difference of Opinion: In case of a difference of opinion between the Lieutenant Governor and his Ministers on any matter, the Lieutenant Governor shall refer it to the President for decision and act according to the decision given by the President.
  • Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2021: This recent amendment further clarified that the expression "Government" referred to in any law to be made by the Legislative Assembly shall mean the Lieutenant Governor, thereby increasing the discretionary powers of the LG.
  • The NCT of Delhi is unique because, unlike other Union Territories (except Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir), it has its own elected Government while remaining under the ultimate control of the Union Government through the Ministry of Home Affairs.

B)
63.21 min  ✓ (Correct Answer)

64.41 min

📌 Shortcut Trick

⇒ Relative speed initially = 225 − 180 = 45 m/min

⇒ Time for 1st overtake = 900 / 45 = 20 min

⇒ Speeds reduce by 5% each time ⇒ relative speed also × 0.95

⇒ Times form GP: 20, 20/0.95, 20/(0.95)2

⇒ Total time = 20 + 21.05 + 22.16 = 63.21 min

∴ Answer = 63.21

Alternate Method

Given: Track = 900 m

⇒ Speeds: A = 225, B = 180

Formula: Time = Distance / Relative Speed

⇒ 1st overtake:

⇒ Relative speed = 225 − 180 = 45

⇒ t1 = 900 / 45 = 20 min

⇒ After 1st overtake speeds reduce:

⇒ A = 225 × 0.95 = 213.75

⇒ B = 180 × 0.95 = 171

⇒ Relative speed = 42.75

⇒ t2 = 900 / 42.75 = 21.05 min

Under the Citizenship Act, 1955, which section governs the grant of citizenship when a new territory becomes part of India?
A)
Section 7  ✓ (Correct Answer)

Section 9

Section 6

Section 5
Correct Option: A) Section 7
Explanation:

The correct answer is Section 7.

📌 Key Points

  • Section 7 of the Citizenship Act, 1955, specifically addresses the "Incorporation of Territory." It stipulates that if any foreign territory becomes a part of India, the Central Government has the authority to specify which persons from that territory shall be recognized as citizens of India.
  • Under this provision, the notification of citizenship is issued by the government through an order published in the Official Gazette. This ensures a legal transition for the inhabitants of the newly annexed or merged land.
  • A primary historical example of the application of Section 7 occurred when Pondicherry (now Puducherry) became part of the Indian Union. The Government issued the Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962, to grant Indian citizenship to the residents of that territory.
  • The power granted under this section is absolute for the Central Government, allowing it to determine the date from which such persons shall become citizens based on their connection with the incorporated territory.
  • This section is distinct from other modes of acquisition because it does not require individual applications or specific residency periods like naturalization or registration; it is a collective grant of citizenship based on sovereign territorial change.

📌 Additional Information

  • Section 5 (Citizenship by Registration): This section allows the Central Government to register any person (not being an illegal migrant) as a citizen of India if they belong to certain categories. For instance, a person of Indian origin who is ordinarily resident in India for 7 years before making an application can apply under this section.
  • Section 6 (Citizenship by Naturalization): This pertains to foreigners who have resided in India for a specific duration. The applicant must have resided in India or been in Government Service for at least 12 months immediately preceding the date of application, and for 11 years out of the preceding 14 years.
  • Section 9 (Termination of Citizenship): This section mandates the automatic termination of Indian citizenship if a citizen of India voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country. India does not permit dual citizenship, except for the Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) scheme which provides limited rights.
  • The Citizenship Act, 1955: This is the comprehensive Act passed by the Parliament to provide for the acquisition and determination of Indian citizenship. It has been amended several times, most notably in 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, and 2019.
  • Constitutional Framework: While Articles 5 to 11 of the Constitution of India deal with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950), Article 11 specifically empowers Parliament to make any provision regarding the acquisition and termination of citizenship.

📌 Important Points

  • Modes of Acquisition: The 1955 Act prescribes five ways to acquire citizenship: Birth (Section 3), Descent (Section 4), Registration (Section 5), Naturalization (Section 6), and Incorporation of Territory (Section 7).
  • Loss of Citizenship: The Act mentions three ways an individual can lose their citizenship: Renunciation (voluntary giving up), Termination (by operation of law upon acquiring another nationality), and Deprivation (compulsory termination by the Central Government on grounds of fraud, disloyalty to the Constitution, or illegal conduct).
  • Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA) 2019: This amendment sought to provide a faster path to citizenship for persecuted minorities (Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians) from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014.
  • Nodal Ministry: The Ministry of Home Affairs is the primary organisation responsible for matters related to citizenship and the implementation of the Citizenship Act in India.

Which substance is the primary component of the reddish-brown coating formed on iron articles exposed to moist air?
A)
Iron Sulfate (FeSO4)
B)
Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide (Fe2O3&middot;xH2O)  ✓ (Correct Answer)
C)
Iron (II) Oxide (FeO)
D)
Iron (III) Hydroxide (Fe(OH)3)
Correct Option: B) Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide (Fe2O3&middot;xH2O)
Explanation:
The correct answer is Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide (Fe2O3·xH2O).

📌 Key Points

  • Rusting is a specific type of corrosion that occurs when iron or iron-based alloys are exposed to oxygen and moisture (water) for a prolonged period.
  • The reddish-brown coating formed during this process is chemically known as Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide, represented by the chemical formula Fe2O3·xH2O.
  • The process of rusting is an oxidation reaction where iron metal loses electrons to oxygen in the presence of water. The overall chemical equation can be simplified as: 4Fe + 3O2 + 6H2O → 4Fe(OH)3, which then dehydrates to form Fe2O3·nH2O.
  • Unlike the protective oxide layers formed on metals like aluminum, rust is porous and flaky. It does not stick firmly to the surface, allowing the oxidation process to continue deep into the metal until the object is completely eaten away.
  • For rusting to occur, both air (oxygen) and moisture (water) are essential requirements. If either is absent, the reddish-brown coating will not form on the iron article.

📌 Additional Information

  • Methods of Prevention:
    • Galvanization: This is the process of applying a protective zinc coating to iron or steel to prevent rusting. Zinc is more reactive than iron and acts as a sacrificial anode.
    • Alloying: Mixing iron with other elements like chromium, nickel, and manganese produces stainless steel, which is highly resistant to corrosion.
    • Painting and Greasing: Applying a layer of paint, oil, or grease creates a physical barrier that prevents oxygen and moisture from coming into contact with the metal surface.
  • Other Iron Oxides:
    • Iron (II) Oxide (FeO): Also known as ferrous oxide, it is a black-colored powder that is not the primary component of common rust.
    • Iron (II, III) Oxide (Fe3O4): Naturally occurring as the mineral magnetite, it is often found as a black scale on iron heated to high temperatures.
  • Factors Accelerating Rusting:
    • Saltwater: The presence of electrolytes (salts) in water increases the speed of the electrochemical reaction involved in rusting, which is why ships and coastal structures corrode faster.
    • Acidity: Lower pH levels (acidic environments) increase the rate of corrosion by providing more hydrogen ions for the reaction.

📌 Important Points

  • Rusting is considered a chemical change because it results in the formation of a new substance, Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide, which has different properties than the original iron.
  • In the Periodic Table, iron (Fe) is a transition metal with the atomic number 26. It is one of the most widely used metals in construction, machinery, and infrastructure due to its strength.
  • The economic impact of corrosion is significant; it is estimated that countries spend approximately 3% to 4% of their Gross Domestic Product (GDP) annually on replacing corroded parts and prevention measures.
  • Hydrated Iron (III) Oxide is also used as a pigment in various industries, known as Pigment Brown 6 or Pigment Red 101, due to its distinct color and stability.

Question No. 12Test (Q.12)
The waves which can propagate in gases are:
A)
Transverse Waves
B)
Stationary Waves
C)
Longitudinal Waves  ✓ (Correct Answer)
D)
Gravity Waves
Correct Option: C) Longitudinal Waves
Explanation:
The correct answer is Longitudinal Waves.

📌 Key Points

  • Longitudinal Waves: These are mechanical waves in which the displacement of the medium is in the same direction as, or the opposite direction to, the direction of propagation of the wave. In gases, waves propagate through compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure). Because gases possess volume elasticity (or Bulk Modulus), they can support the transmission of these pressure variations. Hence, statement 3 is correct.
  • Transverse Waves: In these waves, the particles of the medium move perpendicular to the direction of the wave's propagation. These waves require a medium with shear strength or rigidity to propagate. Since gases (and liquids) do not have shear modulus and cannot sustain a shearing force, transverse mechanical waves cannot propagate through the bulk of a gas. Hence, statement 1 is incorrect.
  • Stationary Waves: Also known as standing waves, these are formed by the superposition of two waves of the same frequency and amplitude traveling in opposite directions. While they can be observed in gas-filled cavities (like organ pipes), they do not "propagate" energy from one location to another in the traditional sense, as the wave profile remains stationary in space. Hence, statement 2 is incorrect.
  • Gravity Waves: These are waves generated in a fluid medium or at the interface between two media (like the atmosphere and ocean) when the force of gravity or buoyancy tries to restore equilibrium. While they exist in the atmosphere, they are distinct from the standard acoustic waves that propagate through the bulk of the gas via molecular collisions. Hence, statement 4 is incorrect.

📌 Additional Information

  • Properties of Sound in Gases: Sound is the most common example of a longitudinal wave. The speed of sound in a gas depends on the temperature, density, and molecular structure of the gas. At 20 degrees Celsius, the speed of sound in dry air is approximately 343 meters per second.
  • Elasticity and Density: The speed of a longitudinal wave in a medium is determined by the formula v = √(B/ρ), where B is the Bulk Modulus and ρ is the density. Gases have a lower Bulk Modulus compared to solids and liquids, which is why sound generally travels slower in gases than in water or steel.
  • Newton's Formula and Laplace Correction: In 1687, Sir Isaac Newton assumed sound propagation in gases was an isothermal process. However, this led to inaccurate results. In 1816, Pierre-Simon Laplace corrected this by treating the process as adiabatic, as heat does not have enough time to flow between compressions and rarefactions.
  • Electromagnetic Waves vs. Mechanical Waves: It is important to note that light (an electromagnetic wave) is transverse in nature and can travel through a vacuum or gases. However, the question pertains to mechanical waves that require the gas molecules as a medium for propagation.

📌 Important Points

  • Fluid Dynamics: Since both liquids and gases are classified as fluids, they share the characteristic of being unable to support transverse mechanical waves in their interior. Only longitudinal waves can travel through the interior of any fluid.
  • Seismic Waves: In Geophysics, P-waves (Primary waves) are longitudinal and can travel through the Earth's liquid outer core, whereas S-waves (Secondary waves) are transverse and cannot, which provides evidence for the liquid nature of the core.
  • Molecular Collisions: In a gas, the mean free path (the average distance a molecule travels before colliding) affects the propagation of high-frequency ultrasonic waves. If the wavelength becomes comparable to the mean free path, the wave can no longer propagate effectively.

Explanation:
The correct answer is Law of Segregation.

📌 Key Points

  • The Law of Segregation, also known as Mendel's First Law, states that during the production of gametes, the two copies of each hereditary factor (alleles) segregate so that offspring acquire one factor from each parent.
  • This law explains that in a diploid organism, the two alleles for a specific trait separate during the process of meiosis (specifically Anaphase I), ensuring that each gamete (egg or sperm) carries only one allele for each gene.
  • Gregor Mendel formulated this principle after performing monohybrid cross experiments with Pisum sativum (garden pea) plants in the mid-19th century, specifically around 1865.
  • The law is based on the observation that recessive traits, which might be "hidden" in the F1 generation, reappear in the F2 generation in a specific 3:1 phenotypic ratio. Hence, the statement regarding the separation of alleles is explained by the Law of Segregation.
  • This principle is fundamental to understanding how genetic diversity is maintained and how traits are passed from one generation to the next without blending.

📌 Additional Information

  • Law of Dominance:
    • This law states that in a heterozygote, one allele will conceal the presence of another allele for the same characteristic. Rather than both alleles contributing to a phenotype, the dominant allele will be expressed exclusively.
    • The recessive allele will remain "latent" but will be transmitted to offspring in the same manner as the dominant allele. The recessive trait will only be expressed by offspring that have two copies of this allele (homozygous recessive).
  • Law of Independent Assortment:
    • Known as Mendel's Second Law, it states that the alleles of two (or more) different genes get sorted into gametes independently of one another.
    • In other words, the allele a gamete receives for one gene does not influence the allele received for another gene. This is typically observed in dihybrid crosses and applies to genes located on different chromosomes.
    • This law results in a typical 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation.
  • Gregor Mendel:
    • Known as the "Father of Genetics", he was an Austrian monk who conducted his famous experiments in the Brno monastery (now in the Czech Republic) between 1856 and 1863.
    • He tracked 7 distinct characteristics of pea plants, including seed shape, seed color, flower color, and stem height.

📌 Important Points

  • Exceptions to Mendel's Laws:
    • Incomplete Dominance: Where the phenotype of the offspring is a blend of the parents (e.g., pink flowers in Mirabilis jalapa from red and white parents).
    • Codominance: Where both alleles are expressed equally in the phenotype, such as the ABO blood group system in humans.
    • Linkage: Genes that are located very close to each other on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, which violates the Law of Independent Assortment.
  • Genotype vs. Phenotype:
    • Genotype refers to the genetic makeup (e.g., Tt), while Phenotype refers to the physical appearance (e.g., Tall).
    • A Punnett Square is a common tool used to predict the genotypes and phenotypes of offspring based on the Law of Segregation.
