Explanation:

Removed part = 45° out of 360° ⇒ removed = 1/8

Remaining volume = 7/8 of cylinder

Total volume = πr2h = π × 122 × 25 = 3600π

Required = 7/8 × 3600π = 3150π

∴ Answer = 3150π

Given:

Formula:

Volume of cylinder = πr2h

Sector volume = (θ/360) × πr2h

Calculations:

⇒ Total volume = π × 122 × 25 = 3600π

⇒ Removed volume = (45/360) × 3600π = 1/8 × 3600π = 450π

⇒ Remaining = 3600π − 450π = 3150π

Final Answer: 3150π cm3

Question No. 3Test (Q.3)
Which of the following is correct about Hibernation mode in computers?
A)
Uses maximum power
B)
Uses minimum power  ✓ (Correct Answer)
C)
Uses supreme power
D)
Uses extreme power
Correct Option: B) Uses minimum power
Explanation:

The correct answer is Uses minimum power.

📌 Key Points

  • Hibernation is a specific power management mode in computer operating systems that saves the current state of the system to the Hard Disk or Solid State Drive (SSD) before completely powering off.
  • Unlike Sleep Mode, which keeps the system state in the Random Access Memory (RAM) and requires a continuous supply of electricity, Hibernation allows the computer to be fully turned off, meaning it uses minimum power (essentially zero) while preserving open documents and running applications. Hence, the statement regarding minimum power is correct.
  • When a computer enters Hibernation, it writes the contents of its RAM into a file on the storage drive, often named hiberfil.sys in Windows environments.
  • This mode is particularly useful for laptop users who need to save their battery life for an extended period without losing their active workspace or closing their programs.
  • The primary function of Hibernation is to provide a middle ground between Sleep Mode (fast wake-up but uses battery) and Shut Down (no power use but loses all open work).

📌 Additional Information

  • Sleep Mode vs. Hibernation:
    • In Sleep Mode, the computer enters a low-power state, but the RAM remains powered to keep the data "volatile." This allows for a near-instant wake-up time, usually within 2 to 5 seconds.
    • In Hibernation, the wake-up time is longer (typically 20 to 40 seconds) because the system must read the saved data back from the Hard Drive into the RAM.
  • Hybrid Sleep:
    • This is a combination of Sleep and Hibernation designed primarily for desktop computers. It saves the state to both the RAM and the Hard Disk, ensuring that if a power failure occurs, the data is still safe on the disk.
  • Storage Requirements:
    • To enable Hibernation, the computer must have enough free disk space to accommodate a file equal to the size of the installed RAM (e.g., if you have 8GB of RAM, you need at least 8GB of disk space).
  • Historical Context:
    • The Hibernate feature became widely popular with the release of Windows 95 (OSR2) and Windows 98, significantly improving mobile computing efficiency for early notebook users.

📌 Important Points

  • Power Outages: One of the greatest features of Hibernation is that it protects your data against unexpected power loss. Since the state is stored on a non-volatile storage medium, even if the battery dies or the plug is pulled, your data remains intact.
  • Hardware Wear: On older Hard Disk Drives (HDDs), frequent hibernating might involve significant mechanical read/write actions, but on modern SSDs, this process is highly efficient, though it does contribute to the Total Bytes Written (TBW) metric over a number of years.
  • Modern Standby: In newer Windows 10 and Windows 11 devices, a feature called Modern Standby (S0 Low Power Idling) is replacing traditional sleep/hibernate to allow the PC to stay connected to the internet and receive notifications while in a low-power state.
  • When to use: It is recommended to use Hibernation if you will not be using your laptop for more than 2 or 3 hours, or if you want to keep your current state but have no access to a charging port.

  • Impact on French Settlements: Following the defeat at Wandiwash, the French capital of Pondicherry was besieged and fell to the British in January 1761, marking the complete collapse of French political power in India.

This includes permitting women to work during night shifts, which is defined as the period between 7 PM and 6 AM. Hence, the statement regarding women working in night shifts falls under this code.
  • Under this code, the Government mandates that if a woman is required to work at night, the employer must ensure adequate safety, health, and welfare facilities. Furthermore, the prior consent of the woman worker is a mandatory requirement before assigning her to a night shift.
  • This code is a massive reform that consolidates and replaces 13 central labour laws, including the Factories Act, 1948, the Mines Act, 1952, and the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970.

📌 Additional Information

  • The Code on Wages, 2019:
    • This was the first code to be passed by Parliament in August 2019. It replaced four existing laws: the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965, and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976.

    • This code consolidates three functional central labour laws: the Trade Unions Act, 1926, the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946, and the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947.

  • The Code on Social Security, 2020:
    • This code replaces nine laws, including the Employees’ Provident Fund Act, 1952, and the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961.
    • A significant feature of this code is the universalization of social security benefits, extending coverage to unorganized workers, gig workers, and platform workers for the first time in India.
  • Broad Labour Reform Context:
    • The Government of India decided to amalgamate 29 central labour laws into four Labour Codes based on the recommendations of the Second National Commission on Labour (2002).
    • The goal of these reforms is to provide ease of doing business while ensuring worker protection and transparency through digitalization and simplified compliance.

  • Annual Health Check-up: The OSH Code introduces a provision for a free annual health check-up for workers above a certain age (usually 45 years) in specific establishments, enhancing the preventive healthcare framework for the workforce.
  • Inter-State Migrant Workers: The code provides specific benefits for migrant workers, including a journey allowance and the "One Nation, One Ration Card" facility to ensure food security when they move across state boundaries for employment.

Question No. 8Test (Q.8)
An object is placed between the focus and the pole of a concave mirror. The image formed is _______.
A)
virtual and inverted
B)
real and inverted
C)
virtual and upright  ✓ (Correct Answer)
D)
real and upright
Correct Option: C) virtual and upright
Explanation:

The correct answer is virtual and upright.

📌 Key Points

  • When an object is placed between the pole (P) and the principal focus (F) of a concave mirror, the light rays originating from the object diverge after reflection from the mirror surface.
  • Since the reflected rays are diverging, they do not intersect in front of the mirror to form a real image. Instead, when these rays are produced backwards, they appear to meet at a point behind the mirror.
  • The nature of the image formed in this specific case is virtual, meaning it cannot be projected onto a screen.
  • The orientation of the image is upright (also known as erect), which means it is in the same vertical direction as the object.
  • Regarding the size, the image is magnified or enlarged, being significantly larger than the actual object.
  • This is the unique position for a concave mirror where it acts as a magnifying glass, producing a virtual and upright image instead of the usual real and inverted images produced at other positions.

📌 Additional Information

  • Concave Mirror (Converging Mirror):
    • A concave mirror is a spherical mirror with an inward-curving reflecting surface. It is often called a converging mirror because it tends to collect light that falls on it, refocusing parallel incoming rays to a focus.
    • The focal length (f) of a concave mirror is considered negative according to the Cartesian sign convention.
    • The relationship between the radius of curvature (R) and focal length (f) is given by the formula R = 2f.
  • Applications/Uses of this Property:
    • Shaving Mirrors: Men use concave mirrors to see a magnified and upright image of the face for a better shave.
    • Dentists' Mirrors: Dentists use these mirrors to see enlarged images of the teeth of patients while keeping the mirror very close (within the focal length).
    • Makeup Mirrors: Used to provide a clear, upright, and enlarged view of facial features.
  • Image Characteristics at Different Positions:
    • At Infinity: The image is formed at the Focus (F); it is real, inverted, and highly diminished.
    • Beyond C (Center of Curvature): The image is formed between F and C; it is real, inverted, and diminished.
    • At C: The image is formed at C; it is real, inverted, and of the same size as the object.
    • Between C and F: The image is formed beyond C; it is real, inverted, and enlarged.
    • At F: The image is formed at infinity; it is real, inverted, and highly enlarged.

📌 Important Points

  • Real vs Virtual Image: A real image is formed when light rays actually meet at a point after reflection, whereas a virtual image is formed when light rays only appear to diverge from a point. Real images are always inverted in mirrors, while virtual images are always upright.
  • Mirror Formula: The position of the image can be calculated using the mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u, where v is the image distance, u is the object distance, and f is the focal length.
  • Magnification (m): It is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object. For virtual images in a mirror, the magnification value is positive.

    • Located in Gujarat, this region saw various resistances against the British, such as the Vheras uprising between 1818 and 1820.

  • Administrative Changes: Following the unrest, the British were forced to make some administrative adjustments to the revenue collection system in the Sagar-Narmada territories to prevent further mass dissatisfaction.
  • Role of Forest Tribes: The 1842 movement was unique because it bridged the gap between the Bundela ruling class and the tribal Gonds, demonstrating a unified front against colonial rule in the Vindhyan and Satpura ranges.

What trend is noted in the share of agriculture in GDP from 1950&ndash;51 to 2016&ndash;17?
A)
Continuous decline with rebounds

Significant reduction  ✓ (Correct Answer)
C)
Moderate growth with fluctuations

Correct Option: B) Significant reduction
Explanation:

The correct answer is Significant reduction.

📌 Key Points

  • The share of agriculture and allied sectors in India’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) has witnessed a Significant reduction over the decades, dropping from approximately 51.8% in 1950-51 to about 15.4% to 17.4% by 2016-17. This reflects the structural transformation of the Indian economy from agrarian to service-oriented.
  • In the early years of independence (**1950s**), agriculture was the backbone of the economy, providing the majority of national income and employment. However, as the Industrial (Secondary) and Service (Tertiary) sectors grew at much higher rates, the relative contribution of agriculture naturally declined.
  • The trend of Significant reduction does not imply a decrease in total agricultural production; in fact, India’s food grain production increased from about 50 million tonnes in 1950 to over 275 million tonnes by 2016-17. The decline is strictly in terms of its percentage share in the overall GDP.
  • During the Planning Era, especially after the Second Five-Year Plan, the focus shifted toward heavy industries, and later, after the 1991 Liberalization, toward the Service Sector, which accelerated the shrinking share of the primary sector.

📌 Additional Information

  • Sectoral Composition of GDP:
    • The Primary Sector (Agriculture, Mining, Fishing) share has decreased consistently.
    • The Secondary Sector (Manufacturing, Electricity, Construction) saw moderate growth but has remained relatively stagnant compared to services.
    • The Tertiary Sector (Services, Trade, Transport, Communication) has seen an explosive rise, contributing more than 53% of the GDP in 2016-17.
  • Employment vs. GDP Contribution:
    • There is a major disparity in India where agriculture contributes only about 15-17% to GDP but still employs nearly 43-49% of the total workforce as of 2016.
    • This phenomenon is often termed "disguised unemployment," where more people are engaged in agriculture than are actually required for the current level of production.
  • The Green Revolution (1960s):
    • The introduction of High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds, better irrigation, and fertilizers under the leadership of M.S. Swaminathan significantly boosted productivity but did not stop the declining trend of agriculture’s share in GDP relative to other booming sectors.
  • Economic Liberalization 1991:
    • The LPG (Liberalization, Privatization, and Globalization) reforms opened the economy, leading to a surge in Information Technology (IT) and Banking, which further overshadowed the agricultural contribution.

📌 Key Points

  • The discovery of the atomic nucleus was the result of the alpha-particle scattering experiment, famously known as the Gold foil experiment, conducted by Ernest Rutherford and his team in 1911.
  • In this experiment, high-energy alpha particles (doubly charged helium ions) were bombarded onto a very thin sheet of gold foil, which was approximately 1000 atoms thick.
  • Rutherford observed that most of the alpha particles passed straight through the foil without any deflection, leading to the conclusion that most of the space inside an atom is empty.
  • He noticed that a small fraction of the particles were deflected by small angles, and a very tiny number (about 1 in 12,000) rebounded back, suggesting a 180-degree deflection.
  • Based on these observations, Rutherford concluded that the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a very small, dense region at the center, which he named the nucleus. Hence, the statement regarding the discovery via the Gold foil experiment is correct.

📌 Additional Information

  • Cathode Ray Experiment:
    • This experiment was conducted by J.J. Thomson in 1897 using a vacuum tube.
    • It led to the discovery of electrons, the first subatomic particles to be identified.
    • Thomson proved that cathode rays consisted of negatively charged particles, which he originally called "corpuscles."
  • Canal Ray Experiment:
    • Performed by Eugen Goldstein in 1886, this experiment involved the use of a perforated cathode in a discharge tube.
    • He discovered positive rays (canal rays) moving in the opposite direction to cathode rays.
    • This discovery eventually led to the identification of the proton, which carries a positive charge.
  • Oil Drop Experiment:
    • Conducted by Robert Millikan and Harvey Fletcher in 1909 at the University of Chicago.
    • The primary purpose was to measure the elementary electronic charge.
    • They determined the charge of a single electron to be approximately 1.602 x 10^-19 Coulombs.
  • Malleability of Gold:
    • Gold was specifically chosen for Rutherford's experiment because it is the most malleable metal.
    • This property allowed the researchers to create a foil so thin that it was only a few hundred nanometers thick, ensuring that the alpha particles interacted with only a few layers of atoms.

📌 Important Points

    • Following the discovery, Rutherford proposed a nuclear model of the atom, where electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular paths called orbits, similar to planets revolving around the sun.

  • Limitations:
    • A major drawback of Rutherford's model was its inability to explain atomic stability. According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle (the electron) should radiate energy and eventually collapse into the nucleus.
    • This limitation was later addressed by Niels Bohr in 1913 with the introduction of discrete energy levels or shells.

  • A Composite volcano, also known as a stratovolcano, is a tall, conical volcano built up by many layers (strata) of hardened lava, tephra, pumice, and volcanic ash.
  • These volcanoes are characterized by a steep profile and periodic explosive eruptions and effusive eruptions, although some have exhibited collapsed craters called calderas.
  • The magma forming this type of volcano is typically intermediate to high in silica content (andesitic, dacitic, or rhyolitic), which makes it more viscous (thick) than the basaltic magma found in shield volcanoes.
  • Due to high viscosity, the lava travels short distances from the vent before cooling and hardening, which contributes to the conical shape and steep slopes of the stratovolcano.
  • Famous examples of composite volcanoes include Mount Fuji in Japan, Mount Rainier and Mount St. Helens in the United States, and Mount Vesuvius in Italy.
  • These volcanoes are most commonly found along subduction zones, where one tectonic plate is being pushed beneath another, such as along the Pacific Ring of Fire.

📌 Additional Information

  • Shield Volcano:
    • A Shield volcano is a type of volcano usually built almost entirely of fluid basaltic lava flows.
    • They are named for their large size and low profile, resembling a warrior's shield lying on the ground.
    • The low viscosity of the lava allows it to flow over great distances, creating broad, gentle slopes.
    • The Hawaiian Islands are composed of a chain of shield volcanoes, including Mauna Loa and Kilauea.
  • Cinder Cone Volcano:
    • Cinder cones are the simplest type of volcano, built from particles and blobs of congealed lava ejected from a single vent.
    • As the gas-charged lava is blown violently into the air, it breaks into small fragments that solidify and fall as cinders (tephra) around the vent to form a circular or oval cone.
    • Most cinder cones have a bowl-shaped crater at the summit and rarely rise more than a thousand feet above their surroundings.
    • An example is Parícutin in Mexico, which grew out of a cornfield in 1943.

    • A Fissure volcano or vent is a linear volcanic vent through which lava erupts, usually without any explosive activity.

    • Fissure vents can cause large flood basalts and are common in Iceland and along mid-ocean ridges.

Latent heat  ✓ (Correct Answer)
B)
Induced heat
C)
Thermal heat
D)
Specific heat
Correct Option: A) Latent heat
Explanation:

The correct answer is Latent heat.

📌 Key Points

  • Latent heat is defined as the amount of heat energy per unit mass absorbed or released by a substance during a change of state (phase) such as from solid to liquid or liquid to gas, without changing its temperature.
  • The physical significance of latent heat is that the energy supplied is used to overcome the intermolecular forces of attraction between molecules rather than increasing the kinetic energy of the particles. Hence, the temperature remains constant throughout the phase transition.
  • The mathematical formula for latent heat is Q = mL, where Q is the amount of heat transferred, m is the mass of the substance, and L is the specific latent heat.
  • The SI unit of latent heat is Joule per kilogram (J/kg), although it is often expressed in calories per gram (cal/g) in older scientific literature.
  • There are two primary types of latent heat: Latent heat of fusion (involved in melting or freezing) and Latent heat of vaporization (involved in boiling or condensation).

📌 Additional Information

  • Specific Heat: This refers to the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of one unit mass of a substance by one degree Celsius (or one Kelvin). Unlike latent heat, specific heat involves a change in temperature but no change in phase. The SI unit is J/kg·K.
  • Thermal Heat (Internal Energy): Often refers to the total energy contained within a thermodynamic system, including both kinetic energy of molecular motion and potential energy of molecular arrangement.
  • Sensible Heat: This is the thermal energy whose transfer to or from a substance results in a change of temperature that can be "sensed" by a thermometer. This is distinct from latent heat which does not affect the thermometer reading during the change of state.
  • Applications: The concept of latent heat explains why steam at 100 degrees Celsius causes more severe burns than boiling water at the same temperature, as steam carries additional latent heat of vaporization. It also explains how sweating cools the human body through the evaporation of water.

📌 Important Points

  • Latent Heat of Fusion of Ice: For water, the latent heat required to turn ice into liquid water is approximately 3.34 × 10^5 J/kg (or 80 cal/g).
  • Latent Heat of Vaporization of Water: To turn liquid water into steam at its boiling point, the energy required is approximately 22.6 × 10^5 J/kg (or 540 cal/g).
  • Phase Change Direction: When a substance changes from a high-energy state to a lower-energy state (e.g., gas to liquid), latent heat is released into the surroundings. Conversely, absorbing latent heat leads to a higher-energy state.
  • Numerical Value: In calculations, if 1 kg of ice at 0°C is converted to water at 0°C, the numerical value of heat absorbed is exactly equal to the latent heat of fusion.
