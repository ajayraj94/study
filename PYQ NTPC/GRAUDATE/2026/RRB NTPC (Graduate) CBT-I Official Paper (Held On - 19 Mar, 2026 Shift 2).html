Question No. 1Test (Q.1)
What condition must be fulfilled by a person born outside India after December 3, 2004, to be considered an Indian citizen by descent?
A)
Both parents must be Indian citizens.
B)
They must reside in India for at least 5 years.
C)
They must renounce any other nationality.  ✓ (Correct Answer)
D)
They must reside in India for 10 years.
Correct Option: C) They must renounce any other nationality.
Explanation:

The correct answer is They must renounce any other nationality.

📌 Key Points

  • Indian citizenship is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955, which provides various ways to acquire citizenship, including by birth, descent, registration, and naturalization.
  • For persons born outside India on or after December 3, 2004, the rules for citizenship by descent were made more stringent by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003.
  • Under this amendment, a person born outside the country shall not be an Indian citizen unless their birth is registered at an Indian Consulate within one year of the date of birth.
  • Furthermore, the parents of such a minor must provide a declaration that the child does not hold the passport of another country. This essentially means they must not hold (or must effectively renounce) any other nationality to claim Indian citizenship.
  • The Central Government may, however, allow registration after the expiry of the one year period if the parents can show sufficient cause for the delay.

📌 Additional Information

  • Constitutional Provisions: Articles 5 to 11 of Part II of the Constitution of India deal with citizenship. However, these articles only identified persons who became citizens at the commencement of the Constitution on January 26, 1950.
  • Citizenship by Birth:
    • A person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, but before July 1, 1987, is a citizen by birth regardless of the nationality of their parents.
    • From July 1, 1987, to December 2, 2004, a person is a citizen only if at least one parent was an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
    • After December 3, 2004, a person born in India is a citizen only if both parents are Indian citizens or one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant.
  • Citizenship by Registration: The Central Government may register any person (not being an illegal migrant) as a citizen if they belong to categories such as Persons of Indian Origin (PIO) who have been ordinarily resident in India for 7 years.
  • Citizenship by Naturalization: A foreigner can acquire Indian citizenship by naturalization if they have resided in India for 12 years (specifically 11 years in the preceding 14 years plus the 12 months immediately preceding the application).
  • Single Citizenship: Unlike federal systems like the USA, the Indian Constitution provides for single citizenship, meaning there is no separate state citizenship.

📌 Important Points

  • The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal organisation responsible for matters related to citizenship in India.
  • The Parliament derives its power to legislate on citizenship from Article 11 of the Constitution.
  • Loss of Citizenship: The Citizenship Act, 1955 also prescribes three ways of losing citizenship: Renunciation (voluntary), Termination (when another country's citizenship is acquired), and Deprivation (compulsory termination by the government based on fraud or disloyalty).
  • The concept of Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI) was introduced to provide certain rights to the Indian diaspora, but it does not equate to dual citizenship as OCI holders do not have voting rights or the right to hold constitutional posts.

Question No. 7Test (Q.7)
Which authority is empowered under the Constitution to define the powers, privileges, and immunities of the members of each House of Parliament and their committees?
A)
Supreme Court
B)
Speaker of the Lok Sabha
C)
Parliament itself  ✓ (Correct Answer)
D)
President of India
Correct Option: C) Parliament itself
Explanation:

The correct answer is Parliament itself.

📌 Key Points

  • Article 105 of the Constitution of India specifically deals with the powers, privileges, and immunities of the Houses of Parliament and their members and committees.
  • According to Article 105(3), the powers, privileges, and immunities of each House of Parliament, and of the members and the committees of each House, shall be such as may from time to time be defined by Parliament by law. Hence, the statement that Parliament itself is empowered is correct.
  • Until so defined by law, these privileges were to be the same as those of the House of Commons of the Parliament of the United Kingdom as of January 26, 1950.
  • The 44th Amendment Act of 1978 modified this provision to state that the privileges are those which the House, its members, and committees had at the time the amendment came into force, omitting the direct reference to the British House of Commons.
  • While the Supreme Court (Option 1) has the power of judicial review to ensure privileges do not violate Fundamental Rights, it does not define the privileges themselves. Hence, statement 1 is incorrect.
  • The Speaker of the Lok Sabha (Option 2) is the guardian of the privileges and decides on matters of breach of privilege within the House but does not have the constitutional authority to "define" them through legislation. Hence, statement 2 is incorrect.
  • The President of India (Option 4) summons the sessions but does not define the legal scope of parliamentary immunities. Hence, statement 4 is incorrect.

📌 Additional Information

  • Individual Privileges:
    • Freedom of Speech: Under Article 105(1), members have freedom of speech in Parliament. No member is liable to any proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given by him in Parliament or any committee thereof as per Article 105(2).
    • Freedom from Arrest: Members cannot be arrested during the session of Parliament and 40 days before the beginning and 40 days after the end of a session. This privilege is limited to civil cases and does not extend to criminal cases or preventive detention.
    • Exemption from Jury Service: Members can refuse to give evidence and appear as a witness in a case pending in a court when Parliament is in session.
  • Collective Privileges:
    • Right to Publish Proceedings: The House has the right to publish its reports, debates, and proceedings and can also prohibit others from publishing them.
    • Exclusion of Strangers: Each House has the right to exclude strangers from its proceedings and hold secret sittings to discuss important matters.
    • Right to Punish: Parliament has the power to punish members as well as outsiders for breach of privilege or contempt of the House through reprimand, admonition, or imprisonment.
    • Inquiry into Proceedings: The courts are prohibited from inquiring into the proceedings of a House or its committees.
  • State Legislatures:
    • Similar to Article 105, Article 194 of the Constitution of India defines the powers, privileges, and immunities of the State Legislatures, their members, and committees.

📌 Important Points

  • Codification of Privileges: To date, Parliament has not enacted any comprehensive law to codify all its privileges. They remain based largely on British parliamentary conventions and precedents established over time.
  • Privileges vs. Fundamental Rights: In the MSM Sharma case (1959), the Supreme Court held that parliamentary privileges are not subject to Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech). However, in the Keshav Singh case (1965), the court clarified that privileges are subject to Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty).
  • Rule of Law: Despite having vast powers, the exercise of privileges must not be arbitrary and must align with the broader constitutional framework of India.

  • Upon its capture, Goa became the first Indian territory to be under European subordination since the time of Alexander the Great. This established Goa as a permanent Portuguese settlement.
  • Initially, the Portuguese headquarters were located in Cochin (Kochi). However, after the capture and consolidation of power, the capital of the Portuguese State of India (Estado da Índia) was officially shifted to Goa in 1530 by Nino da Cunha.
  • The port of Goa was strategically vital for controlling the trade of Arabian horses and the export of spices, specifically pepper and cinnamon, to Europe.
  • The Portuguese rule in Goa lasted for about 450 years, making it one of the longest colonial possessions in the world. Hence, Goa is the correct answer.

📌 Additional Information

  • Vasco da Gama and Calicut:
    • The Portuguese first arrived in India when Vasco da Gama reached Calicut (Kozhikode) on May 20, 1498. He was received by the local ruler known as the Zamorin.
    • While Calicut was the entry point, the Portuguese faced stiff resistance from Arab traders there, which eventually led them to seek other bases like Goa and Cochin.
  • Francisco de Almeida:
    • He was appointed as the first Governor of the Portuguese in India in 1505.
    • He is famous for the Blue Water Policy (Cartaz System), which aimed at making the Portuguese the masters of the Indian Ocean through naval supremacy rather than land-based forts.
  • Afonso de Albuquerque:
    • Known as the Real Founder of Portuguese power in India, he succeeded Almeida as Governor in 1509.
    • He abolished the practice of Sati in Goa and encouraged Portuguese men to marry Indian women to create a loyal local population.
  • Other Portuguese Settlements:
    • Apart from Goa, they established colonies in Daman, Diu, Salsette, Bassein, and Chaul.
    • The island of Bombay (Mumbai) was also under Portuguese control until 1661, when it was given to King Charles II of England as part of the dowry for Catherine of Braganza.

📌 Important Points

  • End of Portuguese Rule:
    • Goa remained a Portuguese colony even after India gained independence from the British in 1947.
    • The Government of India launched Operation Vijay on December 18, 1961, to liberate Goa, Daman, and Diu.
    • Goa was officially integrated into the Indian Union on December 19, 1961, as a Union Territory and later became a full-fledged State on May 30, 1987.
  • Cultural Impact:
    • The Portuguese introduced the Printing Press in India at Goa in 1556.
    • They also introduced new crops to India, including tobacco, pineapple, cashew, and chilli.
Question No. 13Test (Q.13)
The fourth generation of computers marked the use of integrated circuits and compact computing. This generation began with the invention of which key component?
A)
Microprocessor Chip  ✓ (Correct Answer)
B)
Transistor Device Unit
C)
ENIAC Computer System
D)

Correct Option: A) Microprocessor Chip
Explanation:

The correct answer is Microprocessor Chip.

📌 Key Points

  • The fourth generation of computers (roughly 1971 to 1980) is defined by the development and implementation of the Microprocessor Chip. This technology allowed thousands of integrated circuits to be built onto a single silicon chip. Hence, the statement regarding the Microprocessor Chip is correct.
  • In 1971, the Intel 4004 chip, developed by Ted Hoff and his team, located all the components of the computer—from the central processing unit (CPU) and memory to input/output controls—on a single chip.
  • This era saw the transition from Large Scale Integration (LSI) to Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) technology. VLSI enabled the placement of hundreds of thousands of transistors on a single microchip, drastically reducing the size and cost of computers.
  • The microprocessor moved computers out of the realm of government and large corporations and into the consumer market, leading to the birth of the Personal Computer (PC) and handheld devices.
  • Fourth-generation computers also marked the beginning of networking, leading to the eventual development of the Internet and distributed operating systems.

📌 Additional Information

  • Transistor Device Unit: This component was the hallmark of the second generation of computers (1956–1963). Transistors replaced vacuum tubes, making computers smaller, faster, and cheaper to produce. However, they were still limited compared to later integrated circuits. Hence, this option is incorrect for the fourth generation.
  • ENIAC Computer System: The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer (ENIAC) was the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer, completed in 1945. It belonged to the first generation and used over 18,000 vacuum tubes, occupying roughly 1,800 square feet. Hence, this option is incorrect.
  • Tabulating Machine Device: Invented by Herman Hollerith in the late 1880s, this was an electromechanical machine designed to assist in summarizing information stored on punched cards for the 1890 U.S. Census. It is a precursor to modern computing but does not belong to the electronic four-generation hierarchy. Hence, this option is incorrect.
  • GUI and Software: The fourth generation also saw the rise of Graphical User Interfaces (GUI), the mouse, and advanced operating systems like MS-DOS and Apple Macintosh, which made computing user-friendly for non-specialists.

📌 Important Points

  • Generational Evolution:
    • First Generation (1940-1956): Used Vacuum Tubes.
    • Second Generation (1956-1963): Used Transistors.
    • Third Generation (1964-1971): Used Integrated Circuits (ICs).
    • Fourth Generation (1971-Present/1980): Uses Microprocessors (VLSI).
    • Fifth Generation (Present and Beyond): Uses Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Ultra Large Scale Integration (ULSI).
  • Memory and Storage: Fourth-generation computers utilized semiconductor memory like RAM (Random Access Memory) and ROM (Read-Only Memory), replacing the magnetic core memory used in previous versions. This era also introduced magnetic disks and optical disks for high-capacity storage.
  • Programming Languages: High-level programming languages such as C, C++, and Java were developed or became widely popular during this generation, allowing for more complex and portable software applications across different hardware platforms.
